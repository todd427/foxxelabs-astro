## The Data Behind Ireland's Entry-Level Squeeze

Almost half of Irish employers have reduced the number of entry and graduate-level roles available in their organisations, according to new research from hiring platform IrishJobs. The timing is stark: this structural shift coincides with rising labour costs and increased AI adoption across the Irish economy. For a country that has positioned itself as a global tech hub, this represents a fundamental realignment of how companies build their talent pipelines.

The numbers tell a sobering story. AI-related terms now appear in over 11% of all Irish job postings as of November 2025—nearly three times the share in both the EU and US. This isn't abstract; it's reflecting real hiring decisions. Meta's announcement of 350 job cuts in Ireland just last week, explicitly citing AI as a replacement technology, underscores that this trend extends beyond small policy adjustments into major corporate strategy.

## Why This Matters for Ireland's Labour Market

Ireland's exposure to AI disruption is particularly acute. The country's younger workers and those in highly digitised sectors face the most risk. Unlike some European countries where AI adoption has increased productivity by 4% on average without short-term employment losses, Ireland's labour market dynamics appear different.

Here's the critical distinction: while European research from the Training Foundation (April 2026) emphasises that "job transformation outweighs job loss," Ireland's data suggests the transformation is happening *at entry level*—exactly where career pathways typically begin. This creates a generational risk. When companies skip hiring new graduates in favour of deploying AI systems or retaining experienced workers, they're not just cutting headcount; they're cutting the ladder itself.

## The Skills Paradox

There's an uncomfortable paradox emerging. Workers with advanced AI skills earn 56% more than peers without them. Yet two-thirds of Irish employers believe the skills needed in their industry are rapidly evolving. The gap between what's needed and what's being taught isn't closing through traditional entry-level hiring pathways—it's being addressed through selective hiring and retraining of existing staff.

The European Training Foundation notes that 79% of European firms use algorithmic management tools, but the distribution of benefits is uneven. Medium and large firms—particularly those investing in intangible assets and human capital—see stronger productivity gains. Smaller Irish firms and new entrants to the market may lack this capacity.

## What's Next for Irish Tech Talent?

The practical question facing Irish policymakers, educators, and tech leaders is whether existing apprenticeship, graduate placement, and early-career development programmes can adapt quickly enough. If entry-level hiring continues to contract while AI skills premiums widen, Ireland risks creating a two-tier labour market where either you have advanced AI expertise (rare) or you're competing for increasingly scarce junior positions.

For builders and HR leaders in Ireland: rethink entry-level structures. Consider apprenticeships paired with AI tool adoption. For policymakers: the August 2026 EU AI Act employment compliance deadline may need to include provisions for graduate hiring protections.
