## The Entry-Level Crisis: Why Young Irish Workers Face an AI-Shaped Career Bottleneck

While headlines focus on mass unemployment that hasn't materialised yet, a quieter crisis is unfolding in entry-level hiring. New research from March 2026 reveals a troubling pattern: generative AI adoption is selectively reducing hiring into junior roles, creating what researchers call a "career pathway erosion" that threatens the traditional route from low-wage to high-wage work.

### What's Happening

Entry-level jobs have disproportionately high exposure to AI—not because they're being automated entirely, but because they're being skipped. Evidence from the US shows that when organisations adopt generative AI, hiring into entry-level positions drops, particularly for workers aged 22-25. It's a subtle but devastating shift: roles aren't disappearing; they're being filled by mid-career professionals instead.

Even more concerning, nearly half of the traditional "Gateway" roles—the stepping stones workers use to move from lower-paying to higher-paying positions—are now highly exposed to AI automation or augmentation. Think junior customer service roles, entry-level programming positions, and administrative jobs that typically led to more senior roles within 5-10 years.

### Why This Matters for Ireland and Europe

Ireland's economy, particularly in tech and financial services hubs like Dublin, relies heavily on graduate recruitment and career progression pathways. If those entry points narrow, we're looking at a generation struggling to gain the experience needed for advancement.

The disparity is also stark by region. In advanced economies (where Ireland sits), 60% of jobs are exposed to AI—but entry-level roles face disproportionate pressure. Emerging markets and low-income countries have lower exposure overall, but when they do face AI adoption, it hits entry-level hardest first.

### The Skills Demand Mismatch

Curiously, demand for AI skills has surged—one in 10 job postings in advanced economies now requires at least one new AI-related skill. But here's the catch: AI-related skills have not contributed to employment growth in the way previous technological transitions did. In regions with high demand for AI skills, employment in AI-vulnerable occupations is actually 3.6% lower. It's a paradox: more AI skill demand, fewer jobs overall.

### What This Means for Builders and Organisations

For Irish tech companies and European builders, this is a critical insight. The research suggests that:

- **Graduate recruitment strategies need rethinking.** Companies that maintain robust entry-level programs may create competitive advantages in talent retention and culture.
- **Reskilling investments are urgent.** The pathways workers traditionally used to upskill themselves are closing. More structured, company-led development is now essential.
- **AI adoption choices matter.** How you implement AI—augmenting roles vs. replacing them—will shape your ability to recruit and retain junior talent.

### Open Questions

The research raises important uncertainties: Is this a temporary adjustment phase, or a structural shift? Will new pathways emerge in AI-related roles, or are we facing genuine skill mismatches? And crucially, what policy interventions—if any—should Europe consider to protect entry-level opportunity?

For Irish policymakers and EU regulators, this data suggests the labour market impact of AI may not be about unemployment rates, but about inequality, opportunity, and social mobility. The numbers look fine; the lived experience for a 22-year-old Dublin graduate looking for their first tech role tells a different story.
